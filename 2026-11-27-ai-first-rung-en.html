The 94% figure is not a verdict against AI

AI exposure, falling youth employment and the missing causal link

KOENJAZHES

An August 28 Bank of Korea analysis says jobs held by people aged 15–29 fell by 285,000 between June 2022 and June 2026. Of that decline, 268,000 jobs, or 94%, were in industries with high AI exposure. It is a striking observation, not an estimate that AI directly eliminated 94% of the lost jobs. That distinction also matters when reading News Factory's youth-employment discussion.

Exposure is not actual replacement

AI exposure classifies how susceptible tasks are to the technology's influence. Observing job losses in high-exposure industries differs from showing that firms cut those jobs because of AI. Adoption dates, sales, hiring plans and other cost pressures also need examination. Jobs within the same industry do not all face the same effects.

Age groups moved in different directions

Over the same period, 173,000 of the 230,000 additional jobs held by people in their fifties were in high-exposure industries, the bank reports. This is not a simple picture of technology displacing everyone in the same way. It gives reason to distinguish experienced workers using AI as a complement from entry-level work being automated and new hiring shrinking. The contrast alone still does not prove individual employers' motives.

The ladder was weakening before the technology arrived

The paper says the youth share of new hires had been falling before ChatGPT appeared. Normalization after pandemic hiring, preference for experienced recruits and the expansion of remote work may also matter. Remote-capable tasks overlap with AI-exposed tasks. Omitting the bank's warning that AI's causal effect is difficult to isolate turns a cautious study into a categorical slogan.

What needs protecting is also a way to learn

The bank proposes training that combines AI skills with experienced workers' knowledge, support for career formation and a balance between technology investment and talent development. The editors focus on the learning function of a first job. Reducing junior tasks may save one firm money while weakening an industry's future supply of skilled workers. Restoring yesterday's tasks and designing new routes to learn are different policies. Before praising or condemning technology through the 94% figure, ask what happens to those routes.
