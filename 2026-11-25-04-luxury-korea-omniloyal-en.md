# A Nation of Extravagance Turns Away from Luxury

## The rise of the 'smart, picky shopper' who looks beyond logos for real value

November 25, 2026 | Trend Check Card (Consumer & Retail)

"Turning away from luxury" is not a declaration that every Korean consumer has stopped buying luxury goods. What this article questions is the limit of explaining desire and customers only through logos and purchases at official stores. The actual market is moving in different directions by brand and by category.

In this article, a "smart picky shopper" means a consumer who chooses carefully, weighing not only logo and price but craftsmanship, the experience of use, care and warranty, and the value they personally need. They consider both new and pre-owned products, but they neither buy something merely because it is cheap nor trust it merely because it is expensive. The "rise" we describe is a proposal to pay attention to this way of choosing. It does not mean an official category jointly defined by the three books, nor a new segment whose size has been statistically established.

The people a luxury brand knows best are usually the customers who spent heavily at its official stores. Their purchase records show what they bought and when, their preferred colors and sizes, and whether they merit an invitation to the next event. Yet someone who collects vintage watches, can explain the differences between discontinued bags, and recommends the brand to others may not be on that list. If they bought through resale or auction, the product belongs to the brand, but the point of contact with the customer stays with another business.

This is not merely a story of missing contact details. It is a problem with how brands measure customer value itself. **Revenue at official stores is important evidence of a relationship, but it is not the whole of a customer's relationship with a brand.**

The concept of the "omniloyal" customer, proposed by Lewis Lim in an HBR contribution, looks into this blind spot: how to recognize and build relationships with enthusiasts who buy outside the store but engage deeply with the brand.[1][2] But we must not leap directly from this to the conclusion that "gathering resale customers brings growth." Interest becoming revenue, the cost of official certification, conflicts of interest with existing customers and sales channels, and the boundaries of handling personal data must all be considered together before it becomes a business strategy.

## 1. The question of "Luxury Korea": what are we buying when we buy things

Kim Nan-do's "A Nation of Extravagance, Luxury Korea" was published in 2007. Its publicly available introduction and table of contents show that it divides luxury consumption into four types -- display, envy, fantasy, and conformity -- and that it deals not only with individual psychology but also with marketing, media, credit cards, and social conditions.[13][14] These types must not be used as if they described the proportions of all Korean consumers in 2026 or the results of a recent survey. Here we read them as an older analytical frame for examining consumer motives.

The frame remains useful because beneath the same surface act of "buying something expensive," different desires can be at work. The wish to stand apart from others, the wish not to be looked down on, the hope of becoming a different self, and the wish not to fall out of the group are different stories even when they choose the same logo. We can also consider the pleasure of learning the history and craftsmanship of the product itself. That last reading is not an additional citation of the book's types but this article's own application to today's enthusiast consumption.

At this point, omniloyalty does not become the opposite of conspicuous consumption. The knowledge that recognizes the small differences between vintage watches can itself be a means of expressing one's taste and relating to a particular group. Showing a large logo and choosing a product only insiders recognize are different methods, but the latter is not automatically purer or more rational consumption. Nor can we assume that someone has escaped vanity merely because they use resale.

So the question for a brand is not to judge "who is a real fan." It is to distinguish what each customer wants to obtain: recognition, expertise, price accessibility, or the experience of using a product for a long time. Respond to different desires with a single discount coupon and you lose the chance to understand the relationship. Driving every desire toward ever-larger spending is nothing to boast about as understanding customers either.

## 2. Attachment and self-justification, read through "Emotional Dictatorship"

Kang Jun-man's "Emotional Dictatorship" is introduced in its publicly available description and table of contents as a book that examines human judgment through concepts such as the affect heuristic, cognitive dissonance, the endowment effect, and confirmation bias.[15] This does not make it an empirical report studying luxury consumption specifically. What follows is this article's commentary applying concepts confirmed in the public description to questions of luxury consumption; it is not a fresh excerpt of specific passages from the full text.

That a consumer feels attached to a product they have owned for a long time and that the market prices that object highly are two different matters. Memories and effort may accumulate for the individual, yet a counterparty in a transaction may look first at condition, demand, and serviceability. Ignoring this difference and turning attachment into the basis of a resale price invites disappointment.

Brand loyalty can likewise travel with critical judgment. We should guard against the possibility of taking quality problems lightly because we like the brand, or justifying additional spending because we have already spent a lot. At the same time, diagnosing a buyer's motive as cognitive dissonance or vanity without directly verifying it is another form of premature conclusion. Psychological concepts are tools for refining questions, not labels for sizing up consumers.

The same standard applies to companies. Speaking often of authenticity, craftsmanship, and heritage does not by itself prove actual quality and service. Telling the story of a product and taking responsibility for that product must be distinguished. Even if a consumer begins the relationship through emotion, the company's warranty and response must be verifiable in concrete terms.

This also clarifies the ethical boundary of an omniloyalty strategy. Understanding a fan's attachment is possible, but using the fear of exclusion or the desire for recognition to induce spending beyond one's means cannot be called strengthening a relationship. We must see the difference between "service that recognizes you" and "a structure in which you are recognized only if you buy more."

## 3. "Trend Korea 2027": hospitality and discernment accumulate before revenue

The publisher's public introduction of "Trend Korea 2027" presents the slogan "ALPHA SHEEP," describing people who move first by their own judgment rather than following the herd. Media coverage of the September 30, 2026 launch event introduced "re:tention strategy" for customer retention, "human edge" for human choice and touch, and "eye capital" (the capacity to discern good things) as key themes.[16][17][18] These are forecasts interpreting consumption trends for 2027, not proof of market results or causal relationships that have already occurred.

The first point of contact worth reading alongside omniloyalty is hospitality. Even when a customer's official-store spending is small, an experience in which their questions are respected and they receive the information they need can be the start of a relationship. On the other hand, if a brand sends more invitations but ends conversations in the store according to purchase amounts, the message contradicts itself. If this is an era in which retention matters, the boundary of which customers to retain must be examined too; that is this article's reading.

The second is how the human touch is demonstrated. To speak of an artisan's hand, the difference must be experienced in production, repair, and care. A service that explains the condition of an older product and honestly distinguishes repairs that are possible from those that are not may be less visible than a lavish event, but it can demonstrate a brand's promise concretely.

The third is discernment. As information becomes abundant, the ability to judge products and explain context can become a resource in the relationship. A one-way structure in which only brand staff are experts and customers merely listen is narrow. A brand can also learn from a customer who has studied a particular vintage product for years. That said, being a knowledgeable fan confers no public authority to certify authenticity. Conversations of taste and official certification must be kept separate.

Connecting the three books in one sentence: "Luxury Korea" asks what we seek when we buy expensive things, "Emotional Dictatorship" makes us look back at the emotions and biases that move those judgments, and the public discussion around "Trend Korea 2027" views relationships, human traces, and discernment in the language of competitiveness. This connection is not a joint conclusion by the three books' authors but this article's analysis, reading the concerns of different eras together.

## 4. What omnichannel failed to connect is not channels but people

Omnichannel connects online and offline touchpoints. A customer sees a product in a store and orders it online; their online purchase history informs an in-store consultation. It is an approach that keeps the customer journey unbroken within touchpoints the brand operates or can connect.

Omniloyalty asks a different question: how do we see the people who never entered that network in the first place? Someone may own several of a brand's products and know their history and construction well, yet if the place of purchase was not an official boutique, that purchase history may never reach the brand's customer relationship management (CRM). The criterion Lim distinguishes in the publicly available HBR Korea text is also degree of engagement with the brand rather than simple spending.[1]

This does not mean brands know nothing about every external buyer. A resale buyer may already be connected through other touchpoints such as repairs, product registration, or event participation. CRM differs with the information each company holds and the scope of customer consent. So the practical starting point of this concept is not "we know nothing about external customers" but the check: **"are we judging the whole of our customer base only by the people who appear in our data?"**

Very important customers (VICs) and omniloyal customers are not mutually exclusive identities either. A person who buys heavily at official stores can also be passionate about collecting and research. Conversely, buying a single pre-owned item does not make someone an enthusiast. The moment Lim's concept is turned into yet another permanent grading table, there is a risk of repeating the simplification centered on purchase amounts under a different name.

### Two axes for seeing customers again

In practice, the following two axes can be used as a hypothesis. This is not a classification table transcribed from Lim's original text but an analytical tool this article proposes.

- **High official purchases, high engagement:** core enthusiast customers. Both product access and expert exchange may matter.
- **High official purchases, engagement unverified:** customers who bring large revenue, but whose motives cannot be assumed. Reasons such as gifts, convenience, or preference for a specific product should be checked first.
- **Low official purchases, high engagement:** the group to watch from an omniloyalty perspective. They may want repair knowledge, information on discontinued products, and chances to share taste.
- **Low official purchases, engagement unverified:** a group whose relationship is still shallow or about whom information is lacking. Lowering the threshold for exploration is better than pushing purchases.

Here "unverified" does not mean "uninterested." There can be quiet collectors who post little online. Turning follower counts and posting volume into loyalty scores creates another blind spot.

## 5. Even if revenue recovers, the customer base can narrow

Why redefine the customer now? Market research published by Bain & Company on November 18, 2026 shows that revenue and customer counts can move in different directions.[3]

- The customer base for personal luxury goods: estimated to fall from about 330 million in 2025 to about 315 million in 2026.
- Revenue in the same segment in 2026: about 367 billion euros, with 1-3% growth forecast over the prior year at current exchange rates.
- The pre-owned luxury market: forecast to grow a further 4-6% in 2026.

The first two figures concern the personal luxury goods segment -- bags, watches, apparel, and the like. Mixing them with customer counts and revenue for the total luxury market, which includes hotels and cars, changes the interpretation. The resale growth rate is likewise a forecast for the scope of pre-owned luxury used in the study, not the growth rate of Korea's pre-owned luxury transaction value. All three figures are estimates and forecasts as of publication, not final accounts.

### The "luxury downturn" does not mean every brand is bending at the same speed

Looking at company results makes the market's cracks clearer. In LVMH's third-quarter 2026 announcement, the group's overall organic revenue growth was 1% year on year. Yet fashion and leather goods fell 5%, while watches and jewelry rose 6%.[19] Within the same company, directions differ. It is inaccurate both to say demand for bags has recovered based only on group-wide growth, and to inflate the decline in leather goods into a collapse of all of luxury.

Kering also announced that third-quarter 2026 group revenue fell 1% on a comparable basis. On the same basis, Gucci revenue fell 6% while Kering jewelry rose 11%. The group explained that visitor flows were the main constraint, while saying that conversion rates, average prices, and spending per customer were supportive factors across most brands.[20] This is the company's own analysis, not a conclusion in which every cause has been decomposed and proven by outside research.

LVMH's organic growth and Kering's comparable basis are company-specific measures that each adjust for effects such as exchange rates and consolidation scope. They should not be read as Korean revenue growth rates in won. Third-quarter results and Bain's annual forecast also differ in period and scope, so they are not sources whose figures must match.

Seen this way, the problem of the downturn is not that "nobody buys luxury anymore." It is that consumer choices are diverging by brand and category, and demand in some core categories is weakening. Even if spending per existing high-value customer holds up, the foundation of the business can narrow when the flow of new entrants and visits weakens. The assumption that raising prices further can solve this problem is itself subject to examination. At the same time, this data alone does not establish price increases as the sole cause of the downturn.

## 6. Four different businesses behind the same "resale strategy"

A brand's interest in resale does not mean it must buy and sell pre-owned products directly. Certification, platform partnership, owning a distribution network, and community cooperation differ in the scope of control and responsibility. The verified real cases deserve to be read by that difference.

### (1) Rolex: the brand shoulders certification and warranty

Rolex explains that through its official Certified Pre-Owned programme, watches at least two years old sold at participating official retailers are certified, serviced and inspected, and come with a new two-year international guarantee from the date of sale. This is confirmed in official material from May 2025 and on the current certification process page.[4][5]

The core product is not only the watch. Attached is a service that reduces uncertainty about whether an item is genuine, what condition it is in, and who takes responsibility if something goes wrong. It is a structure that turns the moment a product passes from its first owner to another person into an opportunity for the brand to begin a relationship again.

In return, the words "official certification" also raise expectations. If inspection and repair capability, parts, and warranty fulfillment are not in place, the disappointment returns to the brand in proportion to the logo it lent. The program description Rolex has published shows the scope of the actual service, but those materials alone cannot confirm how many new customers the program acquired or how much profit it made. Certified pre-owned should not be read as a guarantee of principal or of price appreciation.

### (2) Kering: participating in a platform's ecosystem

In its March 2021 announcement of an investment in Vestiaire Collective, Kering disclosed an equity investment of about 5% and participation on the board. The same announcement introduced the "Brand Approved" service launched with Alexander McQueen.[7]

This differs from an approach in which every brand builds its own resale logistics from scratch. It is a choice to create touchpoints with the resale market by cooperating with an existing platform or participating through capital. However, holding a stake in a platform does not mean free use of all of that platform's customer information. The investment relationship, the operation of transactions, and the authority to use customer data must be distinguished.

Also, the roughly 5% is **the stake announced at the time in 2021**. This article does not present it as a current ownership percentage or an investment return. The act of investing can demonstrate the market's importance, but it does not by itself prove the business's profitability or its effect on customer conversion.

### (3) Richemont: keeping specialist distribution capability inside the group

Richemont's official history records the 2018 acquisition of Watchfinder. In the current group introduction as well, Watchfinder is presented as a specialist business buying and selling premium pre-owned watches.[8][9]

Participating in a platform with a minority stake and keeping a specialist business inside the group differ in the scope of control. The latter can be read as a choice to secure a business base that can be involved in authentication, servicing, and transaction operations. At the same time, owning the business means bearing its operating results and risks.

Here too, confusion must be avoided. The fact that Watchfinder handles many brands does not mean every product carries its manufacturer's official warranty. The seller's inspection and guarantee and each manufacturer's official certification are items a buyer must check separately. They cannot be bundled under the same service name as Rolex's certification program.

### (4) Porsche clubs: meeting through the ownership experience rather than transactions

Cars differ from bags and watches in product structure, but they are a case worth consulting when thinking about the relationship after ownership. Porsche's official 2022 explanation introduces the clubs as independent non-profit organizations while describing the brand's support and cooperation. That guidance presents vehicle ownership as a condition of membership.[10]

A February 2025 newsroom item introduced more than 700 clubs and more than 265,000 members.[11] These numbers are the company's description at that time, not a real-time count of current membership. They should not be mixed without dates with the roughly 240,000 in the 2022 material.

What matters more than a large membership is what sits at the center of the relationship. The experience of owning and using the same product, knowledge, and meeting become the content of the relationship. That said, these materials alone do not reveal the share of resale buyers among club members or an effect on repeat purchases. Nor should every member be classified as an omniloyal customer. The existence of the community is a confirmed fact; the interpretation that it can connect the brand with customers outside official sales records is a strategic application.

## 7. Do products really sell better when the brand sells them itself?

This question needs stricter evidence than case introductions. A research manuscript publicly posted on the Harvard Business School website, "Branding the Circular Economy: How Branded Recommerce Drives Consumption of Pre-owned Products," reports a series of experiments comparing direct branded resale with third-party sellers and peer-to-peer transactions.[12]

In the first experiment, participants saw the same pre-owned coat sold by a fictitious apparel brand, with the seller varied across the brand itself, a third-party retailer, and a peer-to-peer platform. The number of participants who passed the attention check and were included in the analysis was 298. The researchers report observing higher purchase intention and perceived quality in the condition where the brand sold directly. In another experiment, when the platform side was also given a quality-assurance signal, the advantage of direct brand selling weakened.

The managerial implication is not that "all you need is the brand name." Consumers may expect a brand that gets involved to take more responsibility for quality, and the difference can shrink when another seller also provides credible assurance. It is possible to interpret the additional value a brand can capture as resting on its ability to reduce uncertainty rather than on the nameplate itself.

However, this result cannot be restated as an actual sales growth rate in Korean luxury stores. The public manuscript includes experiments measuring purchase intention and incentive-aligned choice experiments, but those are not in themselves long-term field verification of profit. The first experiment cited here is also a fictitious apparel brand case, not luxury watches. Because this article has not separately established the manuscript's acceptance status or publication year, we do not call it the latest conclusion definitively published in an academic journal.

The same research also deals with the risk that a bad experience with branded resale returns to the brand's reputation. It further reports that buy-back and trade-in arrangements can raise purchase intention for new products while shortening the expected period of use.[12] Making transactions easy does not necessarily coincide with making products be used longer. Precisely at this point, tension arises between growth strategy and sustainability.

## 8. The economics of resale: three ledgers to read before revenue

Even if there is value in building relationships with customers outside the brand's reach, not every relationship is profitable. The following calculation frame is not an actual company's results but this article's proposal for checking business feasibility.

**First, the transaction ledger.** For pre-owned goods bought directly, subtract from the sale proceeds the purchase price; the costs of authentication, servicing, transport, payment, and returns; and the reserve for warranty fulfillment. For a business that merely connects a platform, the fee and cost structure differs. The full transaction amount a customer paid must not be viewed as the company's own revenue.

**Second, the relationship ledger.** Examine whether consent-based touchpoints secured through events led to actual service use or further relationships. Someone who would have purchased anyway joining the program is different from someone newly purchasing because of it. Without a comparison group and a sufficient observation period where possible, it is easy to mistake attracting already-loyal people for the program's achievement.

**Third, the risk ledger.** Count separately the inventory capital tied up while items wait to sell, unexpected repairs, dispute handling, personal-data management, and the reputational cost of failure. High-value goods do not become simple to operate just because each sale is large.

Turunen and Henninger's 2022 research likewise explains that a pre-owned business requires a separate reverse-logistics system, with work such as collection, sorting, redistribution, and pricing in its wake.[6] It should not be assumed that an organization good at selling new products can manage variation in pre-owned condition and irregular inflows in the same way.

For this reason, the business choice is also a question of sequence. An experiment that hosts an enthusiast event and learns what services are needed and a business that directly buys inventory and guarantees it carry different costs to reverse. There is no reason to choose the heaviest operating model first before confirming that demand for the relationship even exists.

## 9. Cannibalizing new products, or a new entrance?

The hardest question surrounding resale is its relationship with existing revenue. Someone who would have bought a new product had the pre-owned one not existed may shift to resale. Conversely, someone who would not have bought new because of budget or taste may experience the brand for the first time through resale. A collector seeking a discontinued product may not overlap with the current new-product lineup at all.

Which of these effects is larger must be verified according to product, price range, supply, and customer. The experimental research above suggests the possibility that buy-back and trade-in raise purchase intention for new products, but that alone does not resolve cannibalization concerns for every resale business.[12]

Channel conflict also remains. If official retailers handle new-product consultations and inventory while another operator earns revenue from the resale relationship, roles and rewards must be divided. When the customer touchpoints of department stores, importers, manufacturers, and external platforms overlap, who is responsible for which service must be clear first. This is not a report that conflict has occurred at a specific Korean company but a set of interests to examine when introducing the model.

Environmental claims should also be kept in a separate ledger. The result differs depending on whether pre-owned goods actually substitute for new purchases and increase real usage, and how much additional transport and servicing is required.[6] Increasing sales and automatically naming it carbon reduction skips the evidence.

## 10. For Korean department stores and brands: buy questions before inventory

The starting point for domestic application is not absorbing externally purchasing customers into an existing VIP program. It is forming hypotheses about which customers need what. What follows is not an operating plan with results proven at a specific company but an implementation proposal premised on verification.

One can start with knowledge and care services. Open small gatherings that do not presuppose purchases: how to store old leather goods, maintenance cycles for watches, how to read a brand's archives. At the sign-up stage, rather than demanding place of purchase and the prices of owned items, ask first about topics of interest and needed services. Where authentication and repair are needed, guide people to a separate intake and assessment procedure. Attending an event must not mean confirmation of authenticity or an unconditional repair guarantee.

Roles must also be divided. A department store's strength in connecting customers of many brands differs from a manufacturer's strength in holding technical information about its products. A department store hosting an event cannot judge the authenticity of every brand's products. The parties responsible for authentication, for warranty, and for costs and dispute intake should be distinguished so that participants can understand them.

When cooperating with communities, it is important not to treat their organizers as suppliers of customer lists. Plan events together or create public knowledge content, but do not make receiving the membership list a goal. The design should let voluntary participants provide only the information they need and choose the purpose and scope of contact. This is not a conclusion judging the legal adequacy of any individual program but a principle of relationship design; applicable laws and contracts must be reviewed separately at implementation.

### 90 days is a unit of learning, not a declaration of success

In the first 30 days, segment demand through interviews and small-scale sign-ups. An owner who needs repairs and a collector seeking discontinued products may want different services. The goal at this stage is not to grow the number of contacts but to erase wrongly formed hypotheses.

In the next 30 days, run limited service experiments. For example, open a care-method class and an archive tour separately, and record participation, cancellations, inquiries, satisfaction, and the costs invested. Defining evaluation items and comparison methods in advance for each event reduces the temptation to pick out only favorable numbers afterward.

In the final 30 days, examine re-participation and service use and decide whether to continue. This does not mean the repurchase cycle of high-value products can all be observed within 90 days. Long-term purchase effects need longer observation, and in a short experiment it is more honest to confirm operational feasibility and customer requirements first.

## 11. What to check is the quality of relationships, not member counts

The scorecard should carry at minimum separate indicators of different kinds. The following items and formulas are likewise this article's verification proposal.

- **Valid touchpoints:** people who understood the purpose and scope and participated voluntarily. Distinguished from the number of business cards collected indiscriminately.
- **Re-participation:** the share of first-time participants who return. State the observation period and the denominator together.
- **Service performance:** among received inquiries and repairs, the share actually resolved, processing time, complaints and rework. Do not confuse with attendee satisfaction.
- **Incremental performance:** the difference against a comparable non-participating group or a staged-rollout group. To credit the program, examine selection bias and pre-existing purchase propensity.
- **Unit economics:** compare revenue per transaction or profit confirmed in relationships against direct costs. If there is no reliable data, do not manufacture long-term customer value as a number.
- **Trust loss:** consent withdrawals, complaints about unnecessary contact, misunderstanding of certification or warranty. If this item worsens even as revenue grows, the design must be fixed.

Stopping criteria are also needed. For example, if the scope of certification cannot be explained or the service is backlogged beyond what it can handle, stabilizing operations comes before expanding recruitment. A design that makes using the service unnecessarily harder when a customer declines to provide information also deserves review. If a business meant to find passionate customers ends in greater surveillance and higher thresholds, it has become the opposite of its starting point.

## Conclusion. Do not try to own the customer; join the ownership experience

Omniloyalty is not a claim to discard VIPs and favor resale buyers. It is a proposal not to assume we know customers fully from the revenue ledger alone. Official purchases, product ownership, knowledge, recommendations, and community participation can be connected, but they are not the same indicator.

Rolex's certification and warranty, Kering's platform investment, Richemont's acquisition of a specialist business, and Porsche clubs' ownership experience each show different touchpoints. None of them automatically guarantees conversion and profit. What a brand must choose is not a fashionable name but the scope of relationships it can take responsibility for.

From the "smart picky shopper" perspective, real value is neither an unconditionally low price nor the attitude of rejecting luxury itself. It is the judgment to choose a product that fits one's needs, to check the grounds for quality and service, and to weigh what happens after ownership. Omniloyalty is a perspective on brand engagement and the smart picky shopper is an expression about criteria of choice, so the two groups cannot be bundled as the same thing. Yet the two perspectives meet at the point of needing to understand customers who choose products outside the brand's reach and still ask about value and responsibility.

The question is the same for Korean retail. Will you sort more precisely the people who will pay today, or will you create reasons for people who already cherish your products to form a relationship with the brand? There is no need to choose only one. But to begin the latter, better service and clearer responsibility must be prepared before more customer data.
