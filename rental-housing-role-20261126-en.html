Purchase, jeonse and monthly rent all surging: can rental housing be the market's safety net?

KO　EN　JA　ZH　

As purchase prices, jeonse and monthly rent rise together, we tie the state of rental housing, its problems, government plans and overseas cases into one question: what role should rental housing play in the whole housing market?

Rental housing feature, November 26, 2026. This is not investment, purchase or sale advice, and it does not fix future prices.

English edition based on the Korean original. We separate Professor Kim Kyung-min's personal analysis from government statistics, announcements and OECD data. The 2027 jeonse market price is a forecast, not a confirmed price.

One question raised by two kinds of anger

An analysis posted on Facebook on November 21 by Kim Kyung-min, a professor at Seoul National University's Graduate School of Environmental Studies, comes down to one question. In a market where purchase prices, jeonse deposits and monthly rents are all rising, what do tenant households without homes live on? He split today's anger in two. Owners feel the anger of having failed to buy before prices rose further; the unhoused feel the anger that they may never be able to buy. As of November 26 the post had 327 reactions, 55 comments and 47 shares. This article takes the post's question as its starting point, while separating his numbers from official statistics.

The basis for the term "triple surge"

Kim calculates that since January 2023 Seoul apartment purchase prices have risen about 46%, jeonse prices about 39% and monthly rents about 43%. He calls the three markets moving in the same direction a triple surge. These figures are his own analysis published in the post; this article has not independently recomputed them from the underlying statistics. The direction, jeonse and monthly rent rising together, is consistent with official data. In Statistics Korea's consumer price index, jeonse and monthly-rent prices in October 2026 rose 1.4% year on year, the largest gain in three years and ten months, and monthly rent alone rose 1.5%, the most in twelve years and ten months. There is also reporting that the cumulative rise in Seoul apartment monthly rents this year is about double last year's pace.

January 2027: a scenario in which 500 million won becomes 700 million

The core of his warning is a specific moment. Take a household that signed a jeonse lease at the January 2023 low with a 500 million won deposit, then used its one-time renewal right in 2025 to extend once at a 5% increase, 525 million won. At the second renewal point in January 2027, the renewal right is exhausted, so a new contract opens at the market price. Kim estimates that price at about 700 million won, leaving the household needing roughly 175 million won more. He estimates jeonse prices in 18 large Seoul complexes will be 35 to 60% higher in 2027 than in 2023.

This is a scenario built on forecasts, not confirmed transaction prices. The 2027 market price does not exist yet. But the legal structure behind the calculation checks out. The Housing Lease Protection Act limits the renewal right to one use, sets the renewed term at two years, and caps the rent and deposit increase in that renewal at one-twentieth of the agreed amount. When a new contract is signed after that, the cap does not apply. Each household's actual burden depends on whether it exercised the renewal right and on its contract terms, so not every household receives the same bill.

What is confirmed: jeonse is melting into monthly rent

Separate from the post's forecast, market changes are already confirmed. Monthly rent's share of Seoul apartment lease transactions reached about 51% in January through October 2026, surpassing jeonse for the first time on a registered-lease basis. The shift tracks interest rates. On November 26 the Bank of Korea raised its base rate from 3.00% to 3.25%, a 0.25 percentage point hike. The statutory cap on the rate applied when converting a deposit into monthly rent, set by Article 7-2 of the Housing Lease Protection Act and Article 9 of its enforcement decree as the lower of 10% a year and the base rate plus two percentage points, accordingly moved from 5.0% to 5.25%. The cap is not a market rate, and it does not automatically raise the rent on existing monthly-rent contracts. As jeonse loan rates climb, the advantage of raising a large deposit shrinks, and landlords prefer monthly rent over investing the deposit. Supply is thin too. A November 22 MoneyToday report put nationwide apartment jeonse listings at about 22,000, up 5% in three weeks, yet prices stayed firm. Cumulative confirmed jeonse-fraud victims stand at 42,039 as of November, and the government has bought 11,542 affected homes for rental use.

The state of rental housing: an 8% stock

Public rental housing still occupies a modest place in this market. Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Finance and Economy Lee Hyung-il told a parliamentary audit in October that the rental housing stock is about 8% of all homes and that the government targets more than 10%, what he called "10% plus alpha." The long-term public rental stock stood at 1.931 million units at end-2024, 8.4% of all homes. In the OECD's social rental housing series, Korea falls in the band of 2 to 10% of the total housing stock (based on 2020 KOSIS data). The member-country average in the same series is about 7%, and the Netherlands, Austria and Denmark exceed 20%. The OECD series and Korea's domestic long-term public rental stock share of 8.4% differ in definition and reference year, so the two cannot be compared directly. LH supplies and manages about 1.5 million public rental units cumulatively. Its subsidiary, the Housing Management Corporation, handles tenant selection and contracts, deposit and rent adjustment and collection, repairs and defect work, illegal-occupancy enforcement, and community and housing-welfare services for 257,000 of those units.

Problem one: volume and speed

The first problem is quantity. The stock is half or less of European countries above 20%, and waiting times are long. An October audit heard that about 90,000 households are waiting for LH public rental housing while incoming supply equals only 8.3% of the waiting list. The government aims to supply an average of 240,000 public homes a year through 2030, but funding, land and construction capacity must keep pace. The long-term public rental stock grew by an average of 74,000 units a year from 2018 to 2024; reaching the 2030 target of 2.5 million from 1.927 million in 2024 requires about 95,000 a year. The time between announced volume and actual move-in is how the policy is felt.

Problem two: quality and stigma

The second problem is quality. Small floor areas, aging facilities and the stigma of public rental housing have long been cited as reasons households hesitate to move in. The government acknowledges this. The September 21 Housing Stability Plan proposed a new universal public rental type, 190,000 units with eased income requirements, alongside quality improvements such as mid-size floor plans, station-area locations and floor-noise standards. The judgment behind it: public rental housing must become a general market option, not the last resort of a particular class.

Problem three: the funding structure

The third problem is the flow of money. LH has run on cross-subsidization, covering public rental deficits with profits from land development and home sales. Its debt stands at 174 trillion won with about 9,000 staff. The structure has a weakness: when development shrinks, rental funding shakes with it. That is why restructuring talk points to splitting development from housing welfare, but what replaces the cross-subsidy after the split is the biggest open question.

The government's answer 1: the Housing Stability Plan, 1.19 million homes by 2030

The Housing Stability Plan announced on September 21 commits to supplying 1.19 million public homes by 2030, an average of 240,000 a year, about 14% of the country's 8.717 million renting households. By type: 350,000 built rentals, 313,000 purchased rentals, 230,000 jeonse-lease rentals, 52,000 publicly supported private rentals and 243,000 public sales units. The long-term public rental stock would rise from 1.93 million to more than 2.5 million, over 10% of all homes, and a 20-year long-term private rental model would be introduced to widen the stability net over the jeonse and monthly-rent market. A phased expansion of housing benefit eligibility is also included.

The government's answer 2: the fourth regular briefing on November 26

Today's fourth regular housing supply briefing focused on speed. Next year's capital-area public housing recruitment totals 56,000 units, of which 21,000 are rentals, double the recent five-year average and 44% above this year. Public rental recruitment moves from one year before move-in to two and a half years, recruiting 10,000 units nationwide early. Purchase-commitment agreements expand to all private sites; the ministry estimates 38,000 units are eligible. Purchases of completed unsold homes also widen to non-regulated capital-area cities, with bought units supplied as public rentals. Land development time falls from 68 to 37 months.

The government's answer 3: splitting LH, and the Housing Management Corporation

The supplier's structure changes too. Under the September 3 public-institution reform plan, LH splits again after 17 years into a land and housing development arm and an asset and housing-welfare arm, and the total number of public institutions falls from 524 to 415. How the two bodies divide 174 trillion won of debt and about 9,000 staff, and what replaces the cross-subsidy, are the core questions. In a November 26 Q&A the ministry reconfirmed it will announce the LH reform plan within the year and said there is no concern about supply disruption. Where the Housing Management Corporation, which manages 257,000 public rental units, will sit after the split was not confirmed in the official material we reviewed. As an organization dedicated to rental operations, a link to the housing-welfare arm may be discussed, but this article cannot assert it.

What overseas cases say

Countries where rental housing acts as a market safety net share a trait. Vienna holds 220,000 municipal flats and 200,000 limited-profit association flats, with about 500,000 tenants in municipal housing alone. In Singapore most of the population lives in public HDB housing, and the design, from rental toward purchase, built a ladder into ownership. The Netherlands belongs to the group with the OECD's highest social rental shares. The common point: these countries grew rental housing into a general form of housing used by the middle class, not charity for the poor. When public and quasi-public supply is permanently present in the market, it sets a reference for private rents too.

The core: rental housing's role in the whole housing market

Rental housing's role comes down to three things. First, a price safety net: when sufficient public rental supply is permanently in the market, upward pressure on jeonse and monthly rents eases. Second, a ladder of movement: it gives households an intermediate foothold while moving from monthly rent to jeonse to ownership. Third, it covers what the market fails to serve: demand from the young, newlyweds and elderly that private supply does not absorb. But the role is not completed by volume alone. Location, quality and a funding structure that can carry deficits are required together. This article does not recommend buying or selling a home. The policy's success will be judged not by announced volume but by the units actually standing at move-in, and their condition.

What to verify before January 2027

Whether Kim's scenario becomes real depends on the speed of supply from here. Three checkpoints: whether early public rental recruitment turns into actual tenant notices, whether purchase commitments and unsold-home purchases reach the jeonse and monthly-rent market as volume, and how the LH reform plan due within the year designs funding for the rental arm. Turning anger into numbers is the work of the press and researchers, but changing the numbers is, in the end, a timetable of supply and finance.
