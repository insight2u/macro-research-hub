# Mandatory tender offers: the order of selling matters as much as the percentage

November 25, 2026 edition | Economy and finance | Main discussion date: November 24, 2026

When control of a company changes hands, can ordinary shareholders sell on the same terms? That is the starting point for the debate over mandatory tender offers. If a shareholder selling control receives a premium while others cannot join the transaction, shares in the same company do not carry the same opportunity to exit.

The November 24 edition of Maebul Show also discussed purchase percentages and fairness between shareholders. Its criticism needs to be separated from the current state of the proposal. Financial Services Commission documents and reporting through that evening describe discussions about introducing a system and deciding its scope. They do not mean that a final system is already in force guaranteeing every ordinary shareholder a particular price.

## What do 50% and 100% mean?

In its March 20 explanation, the FSC described mandatory tender offers as a policy intended to give ordinary shareholders an opportunity to share the control premium when corporate control changes. At that time, the government proposed delegating the purchase quantity to a presidential decree, with a statutory minimum of 50% plus one share. It said the specific regulatory level had not been finalized.

Materials published by the FSC for its November 13 capital-market policy meeting also presented the debate between 50% plus one share and 100%. An expert presentation on the FSC website described a proposal requiring purchases at the same price up to 50% plus one share when an acquirer obtains at least 25% and becomes the largest shareholder. The National Assembly's September 28 Legislation and Judiciary Committee minutes confirm deliberation and passage, with amendments, of a Capital Markets Act bill introducing mandatory tender offers. A presentation at a policy meeting is not a final promulgated law.

Yonhap's November 24 report at 21:16 said the ruling party's special committee would hold further talks with alternatives including purchases covering all shares left open. Financial News reported the same day that the existing compromise had been held back from a plenary vote while the percentage and method were being reconsidered. Committee passage, plenary passage, promulgation and entry into force are different stages. This article does not establish a final statutory text or effective date beyond the material checked.

## A higher percentage does not guarantee higher proceeds

A broader required offer can give more shareholders an opportunity to sell. It does not mean that every shareholder must sell. The offer price, the shares tendered, the purchase limit and allocation rules determine the result. The final rules still need to be checked.

Buying the controlling stake first and allocating only the remaining purchase quantity to other shareholders differs from allowing shareholders to participate on an equal footing. Even at the same overall purchase percentage, the number of shares ordinary shareholders can sell may depend on who receives priority. The debate therefore concerns price, quantity and sequence together, rather than one number.

Nor can we generalize that an offer covering 100% must increase or must reduce acquisition costs. The transaction price, the buyer's strategy and shareholders' decisions to tender may change together. Treating the scope of an offer as identical to the amount ultimately paid distorts the cost debate.

## What investors should check now

Investors need to distinguish expectations about legislation from actual disclosures. Reporting that an acquisition is under discussion does not confirm a tender offer. Debate about mandatory offers does not mean that the same protection already applies to every change-of-control transaction.

When an offer is announced, check the disclosed buyer, eligible shares, price, offer period, maximum quantity and allocation rules if tenders exceed that limit. The price paid in anticipation of a policy change can differ from the price ultimately available on exit. High expectations and a guarantee of recovering an investment are separate things.

Protecting ordinary shareholders is not simply a choice between stopping deals and allowing them. It concerns who receives what information and who has an opportunity to exit on what terms. Looking beyond the percentage to whether the structure offers equal opportunities is closer to the purpose of the debate.

This is an explanation of a policy proposal, not a recommendation to trade any security. Sources were checked through 00:36 KST on November 25, 2026. The latest legislative status requires a further check.
