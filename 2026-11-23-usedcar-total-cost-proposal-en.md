# A used car's proposed total price still excludes taxes

## The advertised price and the contract price

Two used cars may appear equally priced until the buyer reaches the contract. Charges outside the vehicle price can change the comparison. On November 22, Korea's transport ministry announced proposed subordinate-rule changes requiring online used-car ads to show fees within a total price, alongside an itemized breakdown. Public consultation is scheduled from November 24 through January 4, 2027. As of November 23, an announced proposal must not be described as an already operative new obligation.[1][2]

## What total does not include

The listed components include brokerage fees, registration-application service fees, management costs and performance-and-condition inspection liability insurance premiums, as well as the car's price. There is an important qualification: the ministry's original release excludes taxes and public charges, including acquisition tax. The proposed advertising total is not necessarily the buyer's final tax-inclusive outlay.[1]

Rather than copying the largest number, compare its inclusions and exclusions. A tax and a fee for handling an application are different things. Seeing a registration service fee does not establish that the tax itself is included. Before buying, ask for a quote that separates the proposed disclosure items from additional liabilities.

## A monthly payment is not a complete comparison

For installment advertising, the proposal would require the cash price, each installment amount, the number of payments, the interest rate and the deposit. Where installment amounts or rates are not fixed, their ranges would be shown. A low monthly payment cannot by itself establish a lower total outlay.[1]

The proposal would also clarify that a dealer directly selling a car it owns cannot collect a brokerage fee. It would restrict contract terms excluding specified defect or damages liabilities without sufficient reason. Again, the content of a proposal is different from a legal conclusion automatically applicable to a particular contract today.[1]

## A refund headline is not an enacted right

The linked JTBC report discusses fees and serious-defect refunds as parts of used-car consumer-protection measures. Its refund headline should not be read as proof that an identical cancellation right now covers every used car. The ministry's November 22 release separately identifies serious-defect refunds and seller warranty obligations as measures requiring legislative changes that it plans to pursue. The video is not used here as evidence of current law.[1][3]

The practical question is straightforward: what does the advertised number include, and what may be added at the contract stage? It is worth asking before a new disclosure system takes effect. Specific cancellation or defect remedies still require a separate check of the contract and applicable law.
