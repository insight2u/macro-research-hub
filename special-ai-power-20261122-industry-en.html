A map of the electricity industry: from power plants to the bill

AI's power challenge involves production, networks, prices and contracts

Published 22 November 2026 · Individual source and reporting dates are specified in the text

한국어English简体中文日本語

A data center needs more than a count of power plants. It needs electricity at the right place and time, with an agreed level of reliability and cost. Looking only at generation hides the network. Looking only at the retail tariff hides the fuel, purchases and investment behind that price.

Electricity is both energy and a service delivered through a network. Plants generate it, transmission and distribution facilities connect it to demand, and market operations and contracts organize schedules and payments. These activities are connected, but they are not the same business. That distinction is the starting point for understanding power in the AI era.

1. Distinguishing MW from MWh helps test investment claims

MW describes the scale of power at a moment. MWh measures energy produced or used over time. The US Energy Information Administration similarly distinguishes power, expressed in W, kW and MW, from energy consumption over a period, expressed in Wh and kWh.

For illustration, suppose a facility uses a steady 10MW for two hours. It consumes 20MWh. The figure of 10MW alone does not tell us its annual energy use. That also requires operating hours and the load profile. A plant's installed capacity is not its actual generation either.

The same distinction matters for batteries. A device rated at 10MW with 20MWh of stored energy represents two hours in a simple calculation. Actual usable duration depends on charging and discharging losses, operating limits and reserve margins. An output rating alone cannot establish overnight supply, just as an energy rating alone cannot establish instantaneous power capability.

This is why the feature separates demand forecasts from the connection capacity of individual facilities. A forecast of rising annual consumption does not guarantee a supply date for a particular site.

2. Electricity and money follow different maps

It helps to draw two maps: one for facilities and physical supply, another for markets, contracts and payments.

Role Activity Constraints to examine
Generation Converts nuclear fuel, other fuels, water, wind or sunlight into electricity Fuel procurement, maintenance, operating conditions and availability
Transmission and substations Moves large amounts of power between regions, changes voltage and connects the system Line and substation capacity, congestion, reliability requirements and construction schedules
Distribution Connects individual users through local networks Local capacity, connection works and supply quality
Market operation, dispatch and settlement Coordinates plans and actual operation, measures trades and calculates payments Market rules, network constraints, metering and settlement methods
Retail and customer contracts Supplies customers and bills them under contracts and tariffs Applicable tariffs, contract terms, changing demand and cost recovery

The Korea Power Exchange, or KPX, operates the electricity market and handles functions including bidding, metering, settlement and market monitoring. Participants include generators, retailers, district electricity businesses and direct purchasers. KEPCO is central to transmission, distribution and retail, but it is not KPX. KEPCO's parent company and its generation subsidiaries also have different roles.

It is therefore inaccurate to say that every unit generated in Korea must, without exception, be sold to KEPCO in exactly the same way. KPX's entry guidance distinguishes arrangements such as power purchase agreements for small renewable facilities. Each project still needs to meet the relevant qualifications and rules.

The key is not to confuse a contract with physical supply. A purchase agreement and proof of adequate electricity at a specific location and time are not established by the same evidence.

3. Why wholesale prices do not translate directly into your bill

KPX's published trading procedure is based on the cost-based pool. It forecasts demand, receives bids for available capacity and prepares the day-ahead generation schedule and market price. Reliability scheduling considers changes in availability and system constraints. Real-time dispatch, metering, settlement and payment follow.

This sequence explains why electricity cannot be treated simply as an item sitting in a warehouse. Tomorrow's schedule and actual operating conditions may differ. Maintenance or a constrained line can require changes. The system must consider where power can be supplied and under what conditions, not merely the sum of generating capacity.

The wholesale system marginal price, SMP, is not an all-purpose measure of the industry. Settlement includes elements associated with capacity as well as energy, and revenue also depends on contract type. A movement in SMP does not mean every generator's profit changes in the same direction or by the same amount.

Retail tariffs sit at another level. Wholesale purchasing costs, network investment and operating costs, and customer contracts and tariffs are connected. A low wholesale price in one hour does not necessarily appear unchanged on a customer's bill. Equally, generation and purchase costs can move while retail prices remain unchanged.

This structure also explains why KEPCO's consolidated results must be separated from the parent's separate accounts. The company article that follows examines that distinction.

4. Korea's bottleneck involves location as well as total supply

KPX's October 2026 regional-pricing presentation uses 2025 statistics to show that about 40% of electricity consumption is in the capital region, while about 76% of generating capacity is outside it. One statistic describes consumption; the other describes installed capacity. They cannot be subtracted to calculate a shortage. They do, however, show that demand and generating facilities are not concentrated in the same places.

Even with adequate generating capability nationwide, a constrained transmission corridor can limit additional supply to a demand center. The system may have to use a relatively expensive generator near demand instead of increasing cheaper generation elsewhere. That is not the same as having no power plants. It means the conditions for moving power affect dispatch and cost.

Time matters too. The value of electricity changes between plentiful and constrained hours, even at the same location. More generation, relocating demand, changing consumption schedules and reinforcing the grid can partly substitute for one another, but not completely.

KEPCO's May 2025 long-term transmission and substation plan describes a gap between customers' desired supply dates and the time required to build facilities, compounded by construction delays. It proposes using expected demand that reflects the likelihood of projects proceeding, alongside confirmed demand, and addresses local acceptance, sites and cooperation with local governments. Inclusion in a plan is not evidence of completion.

5. Regional wholesale pricing and industrial retail tariffs are different designs

The first question when reading recent proposals is which market level they address. Wholesale and retail changes can interact, but their customers and regional boundaries are not identical.

Question KPX's regional wholesale-price design Industrial regional retail-tariff design
Main transaction Electricity-market trades and settlement between generation and purchasing participants Retail tariffs for industrial users
Public document October 2026 industry presentation Government public-hearing proposal of August 26, 2026
Regional approach Reflects transmission constraints between the capital and non-capital regions, alongside the existing mainland/Jeju distinction Proposes 11 regions using broad grid areas and balanced-growth considerations
Caution Mainland prices can remain the same during uncongested hours This is an industrial design, not a household tariff table; Jeju is excluded from this proposal

The table compares the designs in those dated documents. It does not establish that final rules or tariff terms have taken effect, or calculate a customer's actual discount. An announced direction and the conditions ultimately applied must be kept separate.

KPX also presents a simulation using conditions from July 2025 through June 2026. It produced different capital/non-capital prices for 2,748 hours and equal prices for 6,012 hours. These are simulated results used to examine the design. They do not mean all historical transactions were settled at those regional prices, or guarantee that the same hours and price gaps will recur after implementation.

Nor should the maximum reduction in an industrial proposal be treated as a guaranteed discount for every business outside the capital region. Regional boundaries, eligibility and final tariff conditions differ. A wholesale price gap cannot simply be converted into a retail discount percentage.

In its November 3 explanation, the ministry described regional market pricing as a way to reflect local supply and demand and improve market efficiency, rather than merely a source of funding for lower industrial tariffs. That does not remove the need to examine generators' changing revenue. Exposure depends on spot-market participation, fixed-price contracts and settlement arrangements. Better price signals and transition costs for existing participants both deserve attention.

6. Opportunities require more specific questions than 'electricity is scarce'

Rising demand is a starting point for industry analysis, not the conclusion. The following questions help distinguish business outcomes.

What remains for equipment suppliers? Demand for transformers, cables and power-conversion equipment does not immediately turn orders into profit or cash. Examine capacity, delivery schedules, raw-material costs, price-adjustment clauses and payment timing. Industry investment does not guarantee high returns for every supplier.

Who provides flexibility, and how are they paid? Storage and demand adjustment can shift consumption or reduce network stress. But output, duration, connection location and recognized compensation must fit together. Technical capability is not the same as a contractual right to payment.

What can large customers actually promise? If a data center or factory can reduce or move its load, establish the limits of that capability. Do not assume all computing can move freely between times and regions. Service commitments, operating constraints and demonstrated response matter to network planning and contracts.

Who funds investment before demand arrives? Transmission and substation projects need sites, local discussions, permits and funding before construction. If demand arrives late, early investment can face delayed recovery. Waiting for complete certainty can instead delay supply. That is why advance investment and the likelihood of demand materializing must be examined together.

These questions are not a stock-buying list. They distinguish the revenue and cost structures of generation, equipment, construction, retail and power services.

Conclusion: making more electricity is not the same as supplying it better

Generation alone does not complete the industry's task. Power must reach the necessary place and time, while contracts distribute costs and risks in a sustainable way.

Separate installed capacity from generation, wholesale settlement from retail tariffs, and plans from completed facilities. Those distinctions reveal what is constrained, who must invest and which conditions need to change behind the large numbers attached to AI demand.
