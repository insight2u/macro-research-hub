Media Mergers and Abolitions Changed Workplaces Before Blocking Coverage

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's judgment looking together at dismissals, mergers and abolitions, and employment restrictions. Control was also etched into personnel and ownership structures.

Press Control Is Not Only a Matter of a Single Line in an Article

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's decision on the 1980 Media Merger/Abolition and Forced Dismissal of Journalists Case summarizes that the new military junta planned press control needed to seize power. Dismissals of critical journalists and mergers and abolitions of media outlets were not isolated coincidences, but forms of control subjected to investigation. Prior to demanding that certain articles be deleted, it changed who would report and which companies would survive.

Beyond the Facade of a Voluntary Decision

The commission explained that political considerations entered into selecting targets for merger or abolition, and that coercive means, including pressure through legal handling mobilizing state agencies if refused, were exercised. It also found that due process was ignored in the process of asset recovery and donation to the state. One can see from this that state coercion does not disappear merely because there was a formal agreement or a company measure.

Disadvantages That Continued After Dismissal

The decision covers the process by which the Defense Security Command compiled lists and delivered them to media outlets, as well as employment restrictions after dismissal. Some dismissed journalists were placed in the Samcheong Education Corps. It was a control that did not stop at losing a job, but also blocked opportunities to work again. This is an issue that narrows the scope of information society can encounter alongside individual reporters' livelihoods. The latter is an editorial analysis based on the investigation decision.

Distinguishing Cultural Control from Personal Rumors

Adding unverified rumors about celebrities' private lives while criticizing media and cultural policies does not make the explanation of state violence more accurate. This article limited its scope to press control confirmed in official investigations. Property changes at individual broadcasters, concrete examples of film censorship, and explanations of the so-called 3S policy will be addressed separately once each primary source is secured.
