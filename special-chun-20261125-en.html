Five Years After Chun Doo-hwan's Death, the Success of a Mutiny Was Not Absolution

From seizing power to state violence, money, and pardon. Reading Chun Doo-hwan's responsibility and the unfinished issue of redress through court rulings and investigative records.

The Death of a Historical Figure and the Time for Accountability

Chun Doo-hwan died on November 23, 2021. Five years have passed since the date of death recorded in the Presidential Archives. However, the fact that a person's life has ended is different from an assessment that accountability for the power he exercised has been settled. Posthumous evaluation cannot remain focused solely on how to refer to the deceased. It must ask which laws were broken in the process of seizing power, whose rights were erased while maintaining that power, and how far the redress of harm has come. This special feature starts not from rumors about personal character, but from acts established in court rulings and public investigations.

The End of the Logic That One Cannot Be Punished Once Power Is Seized

On April 17, 1997, the Supreme Court en banc ruled in Case 96Do3376 that criminal liability can be imposed even when power is seized through military mutiny and insurrection. The issue at stake was not a simple evaluation of past history. The question was whether, if forces that overthrew the constitutional order seized power as a result and established a new regime, that success prevents punishing their prior crimes. The majority opinion did not treat a successful seizure of power as grounds for immunity. Although dissenting opinions are also recorded in the ruling, they must not be misread as the court's final conclusion. Accountability finalized by the dismissal of the appeals must remain central.

12.12: Who Broke the Military Chain of Command

The ruling did not view the arrest of the Army Chief of Staff without due process or presidential approval merely as an unlawful arrest of an individual. It judged the act as rebellion against the President's supreme command authority and the Army Chief of Staff's command authority. Having the power to mobilize troops did not guarantee the legitimacy of deploying them. When the military chain of command collapses, the choices of an armed organization can decide the fate of civil society. Reducing 12.12 to an internal personnel conflict or a victor's decisiveness erases the nature of mutiny confirmed by the ruling. At the same time, the responsibility of participants must be apportioned according to their established roles and acts, not their names or personal connections.

A Superior's Order Does Not Erase Illegality

The Supreme Court distinguished between following a lawful official order and committing a crime under an unlawful order. It held that the illegality of a subordinate's act is not eliminated merely because they followed a superior's unlawful order. This judgment is not a lesson demanding personal courage alone. It leads to the question of needing institutions to review what constitutes a lawful order and organizational rules that allow refusing unlawful ones. This passage is the editorial team's institutional interpretation. It does not mean that the responsibility of every actor on the ground was identical, nor does it attribute unverified orders to specific individuals.

Gwangju: Why Citizens' Resistance Cannot Be Called a Riot

The same ruling addressed the suppression during the May 18 Democratization Movement and the purpose of subverting the constitutional order. What matters is that the actions of citizens who gathered to defend the Constitution and the actions of authorities trying to crush that resistance cannot be described in reverse. The mere outward appearance that public authority was exercised does not make every suppression lawful. The judgment that the purpose and execution of power must be scrutinized restored the proper standing of the state and citizens. This special feature does not finalize the total casualty count of the time or the ultimate commanders of individual mass shootings through a single ruling. Overall responsibility for insurrection and the chain of command and execution for individual acts involve different layers of evidence. Drawing a strict distinction is not meant to diminish responsibility, but to write about already confirmed responsibility so that it cannot be shaken.

Violence Did Not Happen Only in the Plaza

To understand state violence, one must look at everyday control alongside open clashes. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's decision on the 1980 merger and abolition of media outlets and forced dismissal of journalists summarizes that the new military junta planned the dismissals and mergers to control the press and used pressure and coercive means. Employment restrictions following dismissal were an infringement on the right to livelihood that did not end with a single personnel measure. The question of what could be reported simultaneously affected individual reporters' jobs and citizens' right to know. Control that alters both media ownership structures and reporting staff can narrow choices in the public sphere even without direct reporting orders. This final explanation is an editorial analysis based on the investigation decision.

Labels the State Attached to People

In the Samcheong Education Corps victimization case, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission examined forced internment, brutal treatment, and forced labor together with surveillance and stigma after release. The label 'social purification' could not justify warrantless apprehension and detention. The Brothers Home investigation decision likewise addressed the arbitrary detention of people labeled vagrants and the state's responsibility for management and supervision. However, the two incidents did not occur at the same facility or over the same operating period. Brothers Home spanned periods both before and after Chun Doo-hwan's term in office. The time frames and responsibilities of each case must not be merged into one solely because they share the commonality of state violence. When common structures and individual responsibilities are read separately, who the victims were and what rights were taken from them emerge more accurately.

The Death of Park Jong-cheol: Another Violence Created by Cover-Up

Records from the Korea Democracy Foundation organize the course of Park Jong-cheol's death by police torture on January 14, 1987, and the subsequent cover-up, minimization, and exposure. Responsibility for the torture itself and responsibility for trying to hide the truth are connected yet must be distinguished. False explanations silence the victim once again and obscure the direction of the investigation. Behind the emergence of the truth of the incident were those who brought it to light and the collective demands of citizens. Based on the materials secured for this feature, we do not state that Chun Doo-hwan personally ordered every act of torture directly. Even when distinguishing acts committed by state organs under the regime from direct orders by a specific individual, the question of holding accountable the system that operated power does not disappear.

Conviction, Pardon, and Forfeiture Are Different Terms

In the court judgments provided by the National Institute of Korean History, Chun Doo-hwan's sentence of life imprisonment and forfeiture of 220.5 billion won can be confirmed. The record of his release via special pardon in December 1997 must be distinguished from his prior final conviction. Contemporary MBC reporting also explained that enforcement of the forfeiture continues after the pardon. Here, 220.5 billion won is the finalized forfeiture amount, not the unpaid balance as of 2026. Furthermore, assets held by family members or third parties cannot all be automatically treated as the same money. A 2022 Supreme Court ruling addressed the legal limits of enforcement against third parties and enforcement after the offender's death. The timing and legal doctrine of this ruling are not extended here as confirmation of the current status of all assets or recoveries.

Death Is Neither an Apology Nor an Acquittal

The point at which criminal proceedings end and the time for asking about historical responsibility are not always the same. Receiving a pardon does not mean the crimes never happened, nor does death automatically heal victims' wounds. Conversely, criminal liability cannot be imposed on descendants based on bloodline alone. What is needed is not vague guilt by association, but verifying the basis of actions and assets. To discuss victim redress, one must specify whose obligations remain and what laws and institutions exist to enforce those obligations. This special feature does not compress the current unpaid balance, the family's current assets, or the effects of subsequent legislation into a single number without separate verification.

Perpetrators of Terrorism and Political Exploitation Are Different Questions

Even when addressing KAL Flight 858 and the Aung San bombing, the direction of criticism cannot outrun the evidence. A government record relaying the 2007 NIS Truth Commission investigation results on KAL 858 introduces the conclusion that it was committed by North Korean agents and the finding that there were no clues supporting suspicions of fabrication or foreknowledge by the Agency for National Security Planning. Questions criticizing investigative flaws or political exploitation are distinct from that conclusion. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs records the Aung San bombing as a North Korean bomb attack and honors the sacrifice of 17 martyred diplomatic envoys and attendants. One cannot conclude that the regime fabricated an incident solely from the question of whether the regime politically exploited it.

Do Not Confuse Institutional Lineage with Present-Day Responsibility

The National Archives of Korea records the name changes running from the Ilhae Foundation to the Ilhae Research Institute and the Sejong Institute. This is a necessary starting point for questioning the relationship between power and the foundation. However, institutional lineage alone cannot prove illegality by today's institute or its current members. Coercion in fundraising, use of funds, and individual responsibility must be verified separately in hearing, trial, and accounting records. Asking what remained after Chun Doo-hwan's era ended is different from pinning unverified guilt onto present-day people and institutions. The fact that a name continues and the claim that responsibility continues unchanged require different kinds of evidence.

Creative Works Open Questions; Records Define the Scope of Responsibility

The film 12.12: The Day calls to mind the choices and chain of command surrounding the mutiny, while 26 Years asks how the memory of harm remains with the next generation. However, fictional characters and actions are not testimony of actual events. To develop the questions raised by books and films into historical judgment, one must return to actual court rulings and investigative records. The works section of this special feature suggests methods of reading based on bibliographic data and official film information. It is not written as a book review of having read an entire book or a film review re-examining every scene. Nor does it heighten the intensity of criticism by quoting unverified sentences or scenes.

Do Not Create Arbitrary Causation Between Poverty and Crime

Upbringing, military career, and personal connections matter when understanding an individual. However, an explanation claiming someone obsessed over power because they grew up poor is not verified causation. Filling in personal psychology plausibly may make readers feel they understand, but actually proven relationships of command, mobilization, coercion, and profit get pushed aside. This special feature did not use unverified anecdotes from early life or rumors of personal friendships as grounds for criticism. The biographical sketch from the Presidential Archives was used as a chronological backbone, while court rulings were separated as the basis for criminal acts and responsibility. Distinguishing what constituted a person's background from the acts he chose and carried out reveals responsibility more clearly.

Casualty Figures Cannot Be Created by Adding Together Different Tallies

The Gwangju, Samcheong Education Corps, Brothers Home, and torture cases differ in the agencies that recorded the harm, the periods of investigation, and the scope of recognition. What was counted under deaths, disappearances, injuries, or internment experiences can also differ. Combining different numbers under the label 'total victims of the Chun Doo-hwan regime' makes overlap, where one person is included in multiple categories, and omission hard to avoid. It is better not to present statistics whose denominators and periods have not been verified in the records. Being sparing with numbers does not mean minimizing the harm. Showing which lives were damaged in what ways, and how far official decisions recognized them, is the first task of this article.

Truth-Finding and Criminal Rulings Are Not the Same Procedure

The evidence for this special feature includes both criminal court rulings and investigation decisions by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. The two types of records differ in nature. A ruling determining the guilt or innocence and sentence of specific defendants cannot be viewed as a final list of all historical responsibilities of the state. Conversely, the recognition of state responsibility in a public investigation cannot be translated directly into meaning that every person involved received a criminal conviction. In this article, matters judged by the courts and matters established by the commission were attributed to their respective bodies. Only on the basis of that distinction can questions of harm and redress that criminal procedures could not address also be discussed.

Later Records Must Also Be Cross-Checked Against One Another

Even the label 'official record' does not eliminate the possibility of error in every sentence. The drafting date of an investigation decision and the actual decision date can differ, and historical summaries contain sentences that briefly compress the course of events. This article distinguished between decision dates and publication dates, and did not mix the operative holdings of a specific trial with sentences from other cases. Even when a book's publishing blurb simplifies the political context of a pardon, the actual timing of the pardon and release must be verified in separate historical records. The authority of a source is not a reason to stop verifying, but a standard for selecting which material fits which judgment.

Do Not Separate the Politics of Memory from the Place of Victims

Evaluations surrounding Chun Doo-hwan are frequently linked to today's political stances. However, one must guard against calling upon victims to criticize someone and then abandoning concrete questions of victim redress once the debate ends. The scope of record disclosure, restoration of victims' honor and identity, medical care and support, and the feasibility of exercising legal rights are each subjects to be verified. This article alone does not judge that all current redress measures have been fulfilled or that nothing has been fulfilled. Writing about past crimes in established language and verifying the current state of implementation with new materials must go together.

Using One Person's Special Feature as a Standard for Vetting the Next Figure

The reason for placing Chun Doo-hwan as the first record in a series covering both presidents and contenders is not to put all leaders on the same chart of good and evil. It is to examine with the same questions how they gained power, how they used it, and what scrutiny and accountability they accepted for their actions. The career of a former president leaves behind both achievements and responsibilities. For figures who have not yet become president, records of actual governance must be distinguished from records of pledges and statements. One cannot treat unverified suspicions as fact on the grounds of anticipating future power, nor reduce scrutiny based solely on an assessment of potential. Rather than mechanically applying this installment's ruling-centered approach to every figure, we aim to select records suited to each person's public role and establish the same standard of evidence.

The Remaining Task Is Depth of Institutions Rather Than Volume of Memory

Reading these records together makes it difficult to explain state violence solely through one person's eccentric character. Authority to mobilize troops, means to control the press, administration to classify and detain people, organizations maintaining false explanations, and ties linking money and power were interlocked. This is the editorial team's interpretation synthesizing the materials. Preventing recurrence likewise cannot stop at condemning an individual. Questions are needed on controlling unlawful orders, externally monitoring detention and investigation, disclosing records, and institutionally guaranteeing victim redress. The figure of five years since his death is not a stamp closing the books. It is a starting point that does not blur confirmed responsibility while leaving unresolved questions for separate reporting.
