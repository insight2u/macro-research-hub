# More room for infant care does not mean a place is already available

## Read the change from the waiting list

For parents who need a childcare place, a promise to ease regulation is not enough. Materials released by Korea's education ministry on November 22 describe changes to infant-class arrangements in areas with rising demand and to the service options of public childcare centers. What the rules permit and what a family can book locally are different facts.[1][2]

## Infant-only operation comes with conditions

The general rule had been for centers to run both infant and older-child classes. The ministry says the change allows temporary suspension of older-child classes where population inflows from urban or housing development have caused, or are expected to cause, a sharp rise in demand for infant care. This is not unconditional permission for every center to remove its older-child classes.[2]

The change gives providers more room to match places to demand. A release cannot, however, supply the rooms and staff or establish each center's recruitment situation. It offers no measured result showing that waiting lists have already fallen. Families still need to check the ages accepted, capacity and actual waiting list at the relevant center.[2][3]

## Hourly care is an option, not a universal new duty

Hourly childcare has been added to the menu of mandatory-service options for public centers. They are to provide at least two services from the expanded selection of existing vulnerable-childcare services and hourly care. This does not mean every public center must introduce hourly care. The attached application provision also addresses operators selected or reselected after the rule takes effect.[2]

Hourly care lets families caring for a child at home use designated providers by the hour and pay for the time used. A full-time enrollment queue and a short gap in care are different needs. The latter is the purpose of this service. The Korean childcare agency's 2026 promotional video explains the idea of using care when needed; it is not a guide to this amendment or a live booking list.[2][4]

## Practical checks still matter

Two changes can be established: conditional flexibility in class arrangements where demand is concentrated, and a wider service menu for public centers. The government expects better access and shorter queues. Those are expectations, not measured outcomes already achieved.[2][3]

Before relying on the service, check whether the center actually offers it, whether the child's age and required hours fit, and what current charges and booking conditions apply. This article does not guarantee a place or an open application. Permission to expand supply matters only when it becomes care a family can use.
