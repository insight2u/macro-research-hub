Former Rebuilding Korea Party lawmaker · Technology and citizens' control · November 26, 2026

It is easy to introduce Lee Haimin as a former Google professional. The more important question is whose rights anchor the product-design language she brings to public policy. Expertise becomes public value when it explains not only convenience and growth but also how personal information is used, the people displaced by technological change and those priced out of services by telecommunications bills.

She is a former lawmaker in this series. After her appointment as a senior presidential secretary in August 2026, she left the National Assembly and Kim Hyeongyeon succeeded to her proportional-representation seat. Older pages still label her a lawmaker. This profile therefore first distinguishes her current status, then compares parliamentary and electoral records, recent interviews, her account of reading and information-rights criticism.

1. A former lawmaker, not a seat lost through punishment

On August 31, 2026, the National Election Commission announced Kim Hyeongyeon's succession to the proportional seat. YTN reported that it followed the Assembly speaker's notification of Lee's departure and that she had been selected as a senior presidential secretary. This series classifies her as a former member because of that public-office move, not because a criminal judgment removed her.[1][2]

Her surviving Assembly page describes her proportional seat and first-half work on the science and broadcasting committee. It is useful for historical career details, but later departure and succession records take precedence for current membership. The latest NamuWiki entry also lists her presidential-secretariat service; the classification here rests on the official succession notice and contemporaneous reporting, not on the wiki alone.[1][2][3][4]

This is more than a minor label. A legislator's responsibility differs from an executive adviser's. Moving from proposing legislation and questioning government to helping coordinate and implement policy changes the standard by which results should be assessed.

2. Google and Opensurvey: expertise beyond a company name

Her Assembly biography and Yonhap's August 2026 profile describe a computer-science bachelor's degree and computer-engineering master's degree from Sogang University, research work at the Korea Education and Research Information Service, product-manager and senior-product-manager roles at Google, and service as chief product officer at Opensurvey. Yonhap dates her move to Opensurvey to 2022. These roles connect product strategy and development.[3][5]

Product management can help connect technology, user needs and business priorities. Employment at a large company does not supply a correct answer to every public-policy question. A company can choose around customers and revenue; the state must also represent people who use services less, refuse them or bear technology's costs.

The useful measure is how she defines a problem rather than the prestige of an employer. Does she distinguish a technology's name from its purpose, identify who bears risk and gains benefits, and design procedures to reverse violations of rights?

3. Data transfer is not a contest for one good technology

Edaily's interview published on August 30, 2026 took place on August 19, before her nomination. She then described plans to address digital finance, privacy and platforms on the parliamentary National Policy Committee. Publication date, speaking date and roles before and after nomination must remain distinct.[6]

She criticized treating APIs and crawling or scraping as good and evil in data transfer. Her point was to define the purpose and then set impact assessments and guidance appropriate to each method. This is not permission to use any method. A method's label alone does not guarantee safety or legality.[6]

The interview also requires technical care. A way of accessing data for transfer and a format for representing data are not at the same level. Listing them as interchangeable choices creates confusion. Introducing her concern does not require adopting every technical classification in the article as policy guidance.

Actual decisions must include the data subject's authority, handling of authentication, access scope and frequency, third-party disclosure, storage and deletion, and responsibility for errors. Purpose-led design can begin to specify these conditions, but is not itself legal permission to use personal information.

4. Protection by design: from principle to verification

In the same interview, Lee advocated including privacy protection during planning and design rather than inspecting violations only after a service is built. Prevention also requires expertise, staff and budgets alongside a regulator's later sanctions.[6]

ZDNet reported on November 1, 2026 that she described safety and trust at a public forum as foundations of innovation rather than barriers. People should decide how far personal information is used, starting from citizens' rights and social agreement. That position does not set protection against industrial development.[7]

A sound principle is different from implementation. Do citizens have effective rights to refuse and delete? Does refusal impair access to essential services? What happens when learning and combining data infer information not originally expected? Responsibility for incident notification and compensation also matters.

This profile establishes the direction of her remarks through forum reporting, not that every institution changed afterward. Calling safety a foundation creates a responsibility to demonstrate how it works.

5. Information-rights objections are not personal attacks

In May 2026, Korea NGO News reported civil-society opposition to proposals widening uses of personal information beyond the purpose of collection. The concern was that weakening minimal collection and purpose limitation could expose people to discrimination, surveillance and financial harm without knowing where their information was used.[8]

A joint statement published by Korean Womenlink in August also criticized exceptions for using original personal data. It is the groups' legal and policy assessment, not a final court judgment of unconstitutionality. This article does not compare every provision they criticized against final current law.[9]

Nor are these criticisms presented as direct rebuttals to a particular remark by Lee. These sources alone do not establish that she sponsored a particular exception or how she voted. They are important objections within her current field of policy.

A design enabling corporate use may differ from one ensuring citizens' control. Civil-society questions about necessity, less intrusive alternatives, prior review, independent supervision and meaningful refusal form part of the policy environment Lee must answer as an executive adviser. Saying agreement is needed cannot substitute for answering them.

6. Telecommunications tax credits: access expressed through tax policy

Income Tax Act amendment 2211077, proposed while she was a lawmaker, addresses the cost of accessing technology's benefits. Introduced on June 25, 2025 by Lee and 11 others, it seeks an income-tax credit for part of essential telecommunications costs, with preferential rates for digitally vulnerable groups. The official record shows referral and several committee agenda entries; no plenary passage or promulgation appeared within the record checked as of this profile's date.[10]

Electronic Times described the submitted design as 15 percent for ordinary households, 25 percent for digitally vulnerable groups, with an annual ceiling of 200, 000 won. Those are reported provisions of a proposal, not an existing tax benefit anyone can now claim.[11]

The proposal recognizes telecommunications as an essential expense and an access issue. It also raises questions: do people with little or no income-tax liability benefit sufficiently, and how would the credit relate to direct support or better tariffs? This profile did not verify refund mechanics or final amended wording, so it does not declare the proposal necessarily favorable or unfavorable to low-income groups.

What matters beyond the rate is who actually receives how much help. Evaluating a credit as a policy achievement requires revenue costs, benefits by income band and overlap with existing relief. The number of bills introduced cannot replace that assessment.

7. What rereading reveals, rather than a book she authored

This research did not establish a book confidently attributable to Lee as author. Instead, an August 2025 Asia Economy reading interview shows books she connects with political choices. She described repeatedly returning to Dai Houying's Humanity, Ah Humanity! over many years and discovering different meanings as circumstances changed.[12]

The novel must not be presented as Lee's own book. Nor does reading in an interview fully explain her policy choices. It offers clues to values she describes herself. In the same interview, she said retraining and safety nets should help people facing the transition between productivity gains from technology and changes in employment.[12]

This differs from seeing a technology specialist solely as a spokesperson for industry. It asks who gains and who pays while displaced workers seek the next opportunity. Plans described as in preparation in the interview are not enacted laws or confirmed budgets.

For concern for people expressed through reading to become institutions, she also needs to explain the boundary between services that support choice and automation that evaluates and excludes. Can citizens unfamiliar with technology, or unwilling to use it, live without disadvantage?

8. From an expert's explanation to a citizen's decision

Lee's strength is connecting technology with products and services, finance and privacy, jobs and access costs. Her remarks also show that she does not view rules against risk solely as innovation's opposite. Corporate design experience does not, however, automatically deliver the state's obligation to protect rights.

After moving from legislator to executive adviser, she is judged by coordination and implementation as well as explanation and proposals. Evidence is needed about who decides uses of personal data, whether communications support reaches those most in need, and whether workers carry transition costs alone. Her next report card should measure citizens' actual control over accepting or refusing services more than the number of new services.
