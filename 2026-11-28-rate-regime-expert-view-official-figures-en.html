The claim that market rates rise even as policy rates fall: one expert's view and a check against official figures

An economist's interview claims set against Federal Reserve, Bank of Korea and Treasury figures and past rate records

KOENJAZHES

In a recent interview on an economics channel, the head of an economic research institute said that the 40-year low-rate era has ended and a mid-rate era of 4 to 5 percent has begun. His main evidence was that government bond yields keep rising even when policy rates are cut. This article presents that claim as one expert's view and checks the figures in the interview against official data and dated press reports.

The main claims in the interview

The expert said that since the 1980s, cuts in the US policy rate had usually lifted asset prices, but recently bond yields have risen even after cuts. He pointed to the retirement of the baby-boom generation: fewer buyers for government bonds, so long-term rates are structurally higher. He thought rates are most likely to stay in the 4 to 5 percent range and could go higher depending on government policy. This is one expert's view, not a settled forecast.

Checking current rate figures

On October 28 the Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.75 to 4.00 percent, with two members favoring a hike (Federal Reserve, Edaily). The US 10-year yield that day was 5.036 percent (Newspim). That matches the interview's 4.0 percent policy rate and 5 percent 10-year yield. The Bank of Korea raised its base rate from 3.00 to 3.25 percent on November 26, with two members dissenting for a hold (Bank of Korea, Yonhap). On November 27 Korean Treasury yields were 3.685 percent for 3 years and 3.832 percent for 10 years (Yonhap). The interview's sequence of a Korean 10-year yield above 4.0 percent followed by a stock decline could not be confirmed here.

Checking past rate figures

The interview said the Fed policy rate was 19.1 percent in 1980. The monthly-average peak of the effective federal funds rate, 19.10 percent, was in June 1981; within 1980 the high was 17.61 percent in April (FRED, cited by Finabase). The year differs. The interview also gave a Korean workers' savings rate of 41.6 percent, which could not be confirmed. A press report says the 3-year account paid 33 percent in 1980 and 23 percent in 1983 (JoongAng Ilbo, 2001-09-26). This article cites only figures that were confirmed.

Gold and long-run returns

On November 27 gold rebounded after hitting its lowest level in more than a year (CNBC). Sources put spot gold at about 3,565 to 3,657 dollars an ounce. The interview's figures of 11 percent a year for gold over 20 years, 13 percent for the Nasdaq and an 11-fold rise in gold priced in won could not be confirmed from official data here. Long-run returns change a great deal with the start and end dates.

Limits and other explanations

Demographics are one of several explanations for higher long-term rates. Budget deficits, the size of bond issuance, the path of monetary policy and inflation expectations also matter. That policy and market rates moved apart in some periods does not mean they will keep doing so. The Bank of Korea itself was split in November, with two members favoring a hold. This article is not investment advice and does not recommend buying, holding or selling any asset.

Scope of verification

Figures in this article were checked against public agency statistics and dated press reports. Figures taken from an article are as reported there and may need further checking against the original data. Interpretations give grounds and limits together and are not settled forecasts.
