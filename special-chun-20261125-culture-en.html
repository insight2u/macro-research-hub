SPECIAL NEWS · 02/10

After enjoying the dictator as protagonist, will we forget the victims?

If the ruler’s tactics and dialogue remain memorable while citizens’ deaths become scenery, a story about dictatorship has reversed its priorities. This is not a call to ban art. It is a refusal to consume rebellion as talent and state violence merely as a dramatic device.

Independent editorial criticism · Revised 26 November 2026

한국어 · English · 日本語 · 简体中文

Charisma does not excuse rebellion

Before admiring the manoeuvres of December 12, restore their legal name. The Supreme Court identified military rebellion: armed resistance to the state’s military command authority. A fictional character’s appeal cannot change the unlawfulness of the historical act.[1]

Gwangju is not scenery for a ruler’s story

The Court found the citizens’ protests to be legitimate acts defending the constitutional order. Citizens were agents of resistance, not spectators or passive victims alone. Ending the story with who won power erases what they sought to defend.[1]

Victims’ lives continued after the ending

Surveillance and stigma after Samcheong, and damage to identity and family ties at Brothers Home, cannot be repaired by a satisfying punishment on screen. Return to the commission’s findings and recommendations for care and redress. Do not cite composite characters and invented dialogue as historical records. Reading a work against judgments and investigation findings is our editorial standard.[2][3]
