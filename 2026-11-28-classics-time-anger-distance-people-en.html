Time, anger, distance, people: four classical sentences checked against the sources

Finding the original texts of Seneca, Mencius and Schopenhauer and reading one passage at a time

KOENJAZHES

The wider a short saying travels, the blurrier its author, date and place become. This piece keeps only sentences that can be found in the original works: two from Seneca, one from Mencius and one fable by Schopenhauer. They concern time, anger, people and distance.

Time: not short, but wasted

In On the Brevity of Life, 1.3, Seneca writes: "Non exiguum temporis habemus, sed multum perdidimus." We do not have little time; we waste much of it. He goes on to say that life is long enough for great things if all of it is well used. Before complaining that it is short, look at how it is spent.

Anger: the best remedy is delay

Seneca's On Anger, book 2, chapter 29, opens with "Maximum remedium irae mora est." The greatest remedy for anger is delay. The first impulses are heavy, he says, and they fade if they are made to wait. The same chapter warns against believing reports at once and against growing angry before hearing the person concerned.

People: heaven's timing, earth's advantage, human harmony

Mencius, Gong Sun Chou II, chapter 1, has "天時不如地利,地利不如人和": the timing given by heaven is not equal to the advantage of terrain, and the advantage of terrain is not equal to harmony among people. He gives the example of a city with high walls, deep moats, strong arms and ample grain that is still abandoned. Later comes "得道者多助,失道者寡助": one on the right course has many helpers, one off it has few.

Distance: Schopenhauer's porcupines

In 1851 Schopenhauer published a fable in Parerga und Paralipomena, vol. 2, ch. 31, section 396. On a cold winter day porcupines huddle together for warmth, feel one another's quills and move apart. Cold drives them together again, and they are pricked again, until they find a moderate distance they can bear. He compares this to people, who are driven together by the emptiness of their own inner lives and pushed apart by one another's unpleasant traits. Reading distance as a form of care rather than coldness is our own interpretation.

After reading

The four sentences come from different eras and cultures, yet they share a point: what changes the outcome is less the situation than how it is handled. A short sentence also changes meaning easily when cut from its context, so it is worth reading the passages around these lines in the sources above.

Scope of verification

This piece checks classical texts rather than statistics. Each quoted sentence was matched to the original text in the sources above. The translations and readings are our own and are not the only possible interpretation. Sentences that circulate in quote collections without a source were left out.
