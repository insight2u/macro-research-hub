# Why does tobacco capital keep linking up with cannabis companies: acquisition contracts and regulation's different maps

DailyShot Macro Research | KT&G Special 6

Published November 24, 2026 · Data rechecked: November 24, 2026 KST

Call tobacco and cannabis "a war for the next market" and the story gets simple. But the November 2026 acquisition contract in Canada shows capital combining before competition: Organigram is pursuing a deal to acquire Aurora, and a subsidiary of tobacco company BAT is set to provide funding. It is a case showing the structure through which an established tobacco company participates in an adjacent regulated industry.

From this fact alone, no conclusion follows that cannabis will replace tobacco or that KT&G should make the same investment. Each country's permitted scope differs, medical and recreational markets differ, and a contract is not a completed transaction. This article separates, in turn, the deal's structure as confirmed in public documents, past entry approaches by others, and the boundaries of country-by-country regulation. It does not assert that KT&G's unconfirmed investment amount is zero, nor speculate about the existence of internal reviews or management's intentions.

## 1. What is C$561 million the price of?

Organigram and Aurora announced their acquisition agreement on November 3, 2026. The terms: C$7.80 per Aurora common share, an equity value of about C$561 million on a fully diluted basis. That is the equity value set by the announcement, not the same concept as enterprise value, which includes all debt and cash. Rather than converting to won without an exchange-rate basis to make a bigger number, this article writes the original currency and valuation scope as stated.[1]

Shareholders can choose cash, Organigram shares, or a combination of the two, but proration conditions apply. So it cannot be described as every shareholder receiving the full amount in the way they wanted. The announcement also presented a premium compared with Curaleaf's hostile proposal. But premium figures change with the comparison date and the share-price conversion method, so this is not rewritten as a simple "paid several times the market price" sentence.[1]

The combination of assets the deal aims to create matters too. The two companies said they would combine their Canadian recreational and medical businesses and distribution and production bases in Europe, Australia and elsewhere. That the combined entity would operate five EU-GMP certified production and distribution facilities is also the companies' description of the deal. EU-GMP is a standard related to pharmaceutical manufacturing and quality systems, not a license to sell any product freely in every country.[1]

The companies' projected annual synergies of more than C$50 million should be read the same way. It means they expect savings and expansion effects from cross-selling, purchasing scale, distribution integration and production allocation. It is not yet profit confirmed in post-merger results. The success of a large deal depends less on the sum of announced assets than on integration costs, regulatory approvals and how actual sales follow.

## 2. BAT is not the acquirer but the link of funding and voting rights

The direct acquirer in this deal is Organigram. Writing that BAT bought Aurora directly would change the legal actor. The announcement filed with the SEC explains that BT DE Investments, an indirect wholly owned subsidiary of BAT, will participate in a private placement concurrent with the transaction — a funding plan of £100 million in total, about C$187.5 million at the November 2 exchange rate the announcement used.[1]

An issue price of C$1.33 per share is also stated. However, the structure uses non-voting convertible preferred shares for the portion of BAT-side common-share ownership exceeding 30%. Conversion of the preferred shares is likewise subject to the condition of not exceeding the 30% common-share cap. It shows that economic exposure and voting power are not always the same ratio.[1]

BAT also entered an irrevocable voting undertaking to use the 30% of Organigram voting rights it can exercise in favor of the transaction. That undertaking is a fact that adds force to the deal, but it does not substitute for other shareholders' votes or court and regulatory approvals. A major shareholder's support does not mean the transaction is already complete.[1]

This structure differs from a simple external loan. An existing shareholder puts in funds through new shares and convertible securities, and those funds are used for the cash consideration and related costs of the acquisition. On one side it shares the acquirer's financial burden; on the other it binds the existing shareholder's economic interest more deeply. But a large amount of invested funds alone does not guarantee protection against loss. Both the equity and the convertible securities are subject to the value of the investment target and its terms.

## 3. Not one transaction, but different ways capital entered

The BAT–Organigram relationship predates this deal. A document announced on February 19, 2026 shows a BAT-side private placement of about C$65.2 million tied to the Sanity Group acquisition. That amount includes both acquisition-linked new shares and the exercise of existing additional purchase rights. The transactional relationship between BAT, which holds a stake in Sanity, and Organigram is also described.[2]

Adding up all announced past investments to fix BAT's cumulative cash principal, however, is a separate task. One would have to verify exchanges and reinvestments of existing stakes, whether transactions completed, and double counting. This article does not present an unverified cumulative total. The amounts and terms of the confirmed individual transactions alone are enough to read a structure in which the capital relationship deepens step by step.

Other tobacco companies' past entry approaches differed too. In December 2018, Altria announced a deal investing US$1.8 billion, C$2.4 billion, in Cronos for a 45% stake, with warrants allowing it to raise that to 55% within a set period. Those were the deal terms at the time; they do not mean the stake or the warrants are valid as of 2026.[3]

In July 2019, Imperial announced an agreement to invest C$123 million in Auxly through convertible debentures. The terms then were a three-year maturity, 4% annual interest, and conversion into shares corresponding to 19.9% of then-current capital. Usage rights for vaping technology and research and development cooperation were also included. That, too, was the condition of a 2019 announcement; this article does not write that the same debentures remain in place today.[4]

Equity plus warrants, convertible debentures and staged private placements each carry different structures of risk and influence. That does not allow the simplification that debt is safe and equity is automatically risky. Recoverability, conversion terms, follow-on funding and business value all matter. With the final investment returns of the three approaches uncalculated from public documents alone, this article does not rank which company was the cleverest.

## 4. Canada and Germany: differences inside the single word "legal"

Canada began legalizing and strictly regulating adult recreational cannabis with the Cannabis Act framework taking effect on October 17, 2018. What the government's announcement emphasized was also preventing youth access and displacing the illegal market. Legalization is not a measure that removes every limit on production, sale and possession. Federal and provincial rules and licensing systems must be read together.[5]

Germany's consumer cannabis framework has a different shape. The Federal Ministry of Health's explanation limits adults' private cultivation to three plants per person and distinguishes possession generally at 25g from 50g of dried cannabis at one's residence. Non-profit cultivation associations are also a limited-distribution structure for members' personal consumption. Such a framework cannot be called the same thing as a Canadian-style general commercial sales market.[6]

The medical market and recreational private consumption are yet another scope. That is why medical production and distribution networks are emphasized when explaining the value of assets like Aurora and Sanity. But a company's business description does not replace the latest legal judgment of country-by-country pharmaceutical regulation and import procedures. Whether a specific product is permitted in a given country must be checked by product and use.

So drawing an investment map takes more than splitting country names into green and red. One must ask who may produce, which products may be supplied to whom through which channels, and what procedures exports, imports and research follow. The expression "the market is opening" contains different doors and different locks.

## 5. The United States and Thailand: distinguishing partial changes from current regulation

On April 23, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice announced an order moving FDA-approved marijuana products and medical marijuana products with qualifying state licenses to Schedule III. It is a measure changing how research and some medical products are handled. Describing it as federal legalization of all recreational marijuana oversteps the scope. The broader rescheduling question and the licensing and tax effects for individual businesses must also be checked separately.[7]

Thailand is a case where old travel information turns dangerous. The Korean Embassy in Thailand's May 12, 2026 notice advises that under a Ministry of Public Health ordinance effective April 26, cannabis was redesignated as a Category 5 narcotic. It explains that production, possession and distribution are limited to medical, research and industrial purposes, and that smoking or ingestion for recreational or leisure purposes is entirely prohibited. One must not judge from the 2022 deregulation coverage alone that it can still be used freely today.[8]

The difference between these two countries shows that regulatory change does not always move in one direction. Some uses may open while others remain restricted, and tightening can follow loosening. Past legal change does not guarantee a one-way street favorable to future investment.

For Korean readers there is a separate boundary. The embassy warns that regardless of local regulation, Korean nationals can be punished under Korea's Narcotics Control Act for possessing, purchasing, selling, carrying or smoking cannabis abroad. An article analyzing another country's legal market is not guidance permitting personal purchase or use. This article does not provide ways to use or buy cannabis products.[8]

## 6. The question returning to KT&G is not only "why no investment"

KT&G's November 2026 strategy presentation put forward overseas cigarettes, NGP and health functional foods as three growth pillars. In NGP it described expanding product families such as heated tobacco and nicotine pouches, and set a target of NGP reaching at least 30% of tobacco-business revenue by 2030. That is the disclosed business direction. The absence of a specific cannabis investment in these materials does not prove that the investment amount across all affiliates, funds and past transactions is zero.[9]

Whether management reviewed cannabis, and why it chose or did not choose it, cannot be known without public documents either. Nor can one say from group revenue or operating profit that acquisition funds would be easy to bear. Investment must weigh cash, borrowing, dividends, existing capital expenditure and loss risk together. Revenue is not cash on hand.

So the comparison question needs to change. Which adjacent businesses actually connect to existing technology and distribution networks? Can the laws of the home country and local markets be satisfied? Can expected outcomes be explained against opportunity cost? The BAT case is not evidence that cannabis is a required subject for every tobacco company; it is one example of a possible capital-entry structure.

An analysis that consumer-goods technology and medical supply chains have points of contact is possible. But having points of contact and KT&G holding a competitive advantage in that business are different claims. Checking actually held capabilities, rights and investment terms before a new market's appeal is the order of corporate analysis.

## 7. Conclusion: read the terms longer than the size of the deal

Organigram's acquisition contract for Aurora makes possible the interpretation that tobacco capital does not see cannabis only as a competitor. The BAT side's funding participation and voting undertaking, and the combination of existing medical and recreational supply chains, are the basis. But synergies are estimates and completion is conditional. The expected January 2027 completion in the announcement is the company's schedule, not confirmation of completion.[1]

Aurora's subsequent material-change filing also explains that the transaction requires both companies' shareholder approvals and regulatory and court approvals. The same filing states that closing cannot be earlier than January 6, 2027 and is expected in January 2027. So as of November 24, 2026, this article does not describe it as a completed acquisition.[11] When assessing its later status, the actual votes, approvals and closing filings must be checked again. Even if the expected supply chain is built, the tests of demand, price and integration costs remain after that.

Reading the map of the cannabis industry is not memorizing legalization dates. It is separating a deal's legal actors, equity value from enterprise value, funding from voting rights, medical from recreational, and announcement from completion. Keeping these boundaries makes it possible to describe tobacco companies' adjacent-market strategies without exaggeration. Regulatory change can create new markets; it does not guarantee success for companies or investors.
