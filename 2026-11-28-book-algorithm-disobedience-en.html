What can we recover after we stop scrolling?

A reading guide to Han-na Ju's Algorithm Disobedience, based on an interview and published contents

KOENJAZHES

A new book asks not whether to abandon technology, but whose goals technology serves. Han-na Ju's Algorithm Disobedience places everyday choice at the center. The November 27 News Factory interview and published bibliographic information suggest a question beyond convenience for or against. This is a reading guide based on public material, not a review claiming to have read the entire book.

What can be checked outside the book

The Korean Book Information Center lists a 240-page book published by Dasan Chodang on October 21, 2026. Its contents move from systems that read daily life through changes in relationships and work to experiences of reclaiming slower activities. Promotional descriptions and excerpts are an entrance to a book, not substitutes for its complete argument. Any numerical claims about advertising or psychology require checking the cited studies separately.

Remove the absolutes from the interview

The interview discussed how recommendations may influence choices rather than simply reflect tastes, alongside the explainability of generative models and the difficulty of regulation. The author did not simply say that all control is impossible. She distinguished restricting outputs from explaining precisely why an individual result appeared. Remove that distinction and a technology discussion becomes a contest between optimism and pessimism.

Questions to carry into reading

Changing personal screen habits and changing corporate design through institutions can complement each other, but neither replaces the other. Does an argument place all responsibility on the user's willpower? Does it instead portray every user choice as powerless? Mark the author's experience, findings from other research and policy proposals separately. Using convenience and surrendering responsibility for choices are different things.
