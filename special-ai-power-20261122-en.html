AI consumes power. Korea is betting on time.

The bottleneck beyond semiconductors: from the November 20 AIDC proposals to the grid construction calendar

Published 22 November 2026 · Policy announcement: 20 November

한국어English简体中文日本語

The competition that begins after securing chips

An announcement that GPUs have been secured is not the same event as the day those GPUs receive electricity. Buildings and servers, grid connections, and agreements over local burdens move at different speeds. Reading Korea's AI position only through semiconductor manufacturing misses this gap. Our interpretation is that the next bottleneck is not generation alone, but the ability to supply power of the required quality, in the right place, at the promised time.

What changed this week?

On November 20, 2026, Korea's climate and ICT ministries announced proposed changes to power-system impact assessments for AI data centres, or AIDCs. A draft enforcement decree would exempt qualifying non-capital-region AIDCs with contracted power of 50MW or less. Other planned changes concern non-technical assessments in AIDC special zones and priority assessment for specified projects. The words 'proposed' and 'draft' matter. The announcement identifies March 10, 2027 as the commencement date of the AIDC Special Act. It does not mean all facilities below 50MW are already exempt today.[1][2]

1,000TWh is a scenario, not an observed result

The IEA's World Energy Outlook 2026 projects global data-centre electricity consumption of about 1,000TWh in 2030 in its Stated Policies Scenario, or STEPS. This is neither measured AI-only consumption nor Korean demand. A separate report, Key Questions on Energy and AI, estimates global data-centre consumption at 485TWh in 2025 and projects 950TWh in 2030 in its central outlook. Different documents and scenarios should not be joined into one supposedly certain historical-and-future series.[3][4]

Location and timing are harder questions than growth alone

The existence of generating capacity does not mean a particular data centre can immediately receive its power. Transmission lines, substations and surrounding network conditions must also fit. KEPCO's May 2025 transmission and substation plan covers 2024–2038. It combines phased Honam-to-capital-region HVDC development with reinforcement of the supporting grid. Its page-three first-stage target of 2031 illustrates the time gap. That is a target in the plan, not evidence of completed construction today.[5]

What a law can accelerate, and what it cannot

The national backbone grid expansion law, first effective in September 2025, provides a framework for government coordination, permitting, support and compensation. Article 8 of the current text concerns the national grid expansion committee; Article 12 addresses public notice, inspection and residents' opinions. Faster processing should not be confused with omitting local voices. A law can change procedures, but it cannot simultaneously finish procurement, construction and testing. The legal provisions are facts; the distinction between procedural and physical progress is our analysis.[6][7]

Regional relocation is an agreement, not just a site decision

Policies encouraging non-capital-region locations are more durable if regions are not treated merely as sources of electricity. Local employment and services, additional network costs and residents' burdens belong in the same discussion. The web summary of KIEP's sovereign-AI study also treats Korean AI strategy as a system linking talent, applications and governance with GPUs and data centres. Our conclusion is that facility size should not be mistaken for the performance of an industrial ecosystem.[8]

Five calendars instead of a list of winning stocks

Readers can ask whether announced demand has become a real contract; whether connection terms and dates are fixed; how far permits and local consultation have progressed; whether delivery, construction and commissioning line up; and who carries costs before cash arrives. Growing orders for power equipment do not guarantee identical profit or share-price outcomes across companies. The IEA likewise finds that AI demand does not provide a uniform uplift to the entire energy sector.[4]

Korea needs more than speed

Granting permission quickly and delivering reliable power are different achievements. Attracting a facility and leaving productivity and income in its region are different too. The useful time advantage is not announcing first. It is having contracts, networks and local trust ready together. The next stage of AI investment may be clearer in the quality of these connections than in the chip count alone.
