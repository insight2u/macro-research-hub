How a will became a prize for the world

On this day, November 27 | Between the signature of 1895 and the first awards of 1901

KOENJAZHES

On November 27, 1895, Alfred Nobel signed his third and final will at the Swedish-Norwegian Club in Paris. No Nobel Prize was awarded that day. What existed was a document directing the use of his estate. The first prizes were not awarded until December 10, 1901.

Deciding what the money should do

The will directed that his remaining estate become a fund invested in safe securities. Its interest would finance annual prizes for people who had conferred the greatest benefit to humankind. It divided the interest into five shares: physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, literature and peace. It also named the institutions that would choose the recipients.

The structure mattered as much as the fortune. Rather than distribute the capital once, Nobel linked a fund to recurring awards and specified who would make the judgments. He expressly instructed that nationality should not determine the choice. A private estate would support recognition across national borders.

A signature did not make it work

Implementation was difficult. After his death, some relatives opposed the will, and the named prize-awarding institutions did not initially accept their roles. The official Nobel Peace Prize history describes lengthy negotiations to untangle the legal issues. The Nobel Foundation statutes were approved on June 29, 1900.

A story confined to one great individual misses the work that followed. The estate had to become a fund, institutions needed defined roles, and objections required negotiation. The will was a starting point. Execution and agreement made the institution possible.

More than a simple story of remorse

The accompanying Nobel Peace Center film questions the familiar story that connects dynamite and the Peace Prize through remorse alone. It discusses Nobel’s friendship with Bertha von Suttner and his interests in science, literature and peace. This article distinguishes the purposes recorded in his will from later interpretations of his motives. It does not claim that a single emotion caused the will.

The question that remains

Nobel’s will is not a ready-made plan for present-day policy or philanthropy. A private foundation’s awards and a public budget have different lines of accountability. But one question remains useful: do we describe the rules of implementation as carefully as the promise itself?

Who decides? How is the money maintained? Who corrects a departure from the original purpose? Larger promises need such details. Remembering November 27 is not simply remembering the beginning of a famous prize. It is asking what turns an intention into an institution. This is the editors’ interpretation of the historical record.
